# Lessons from the MrBeast production handbook

"How to Succeed in MrBeast Production" is a 36-page onboarding document
Jimmy Donaldson (MrBeast) wrote for new staff. It leaked in 2024. This
file summarizes the parts that affect packaging (thumbnail and title)
and the retention plan the packaging has to set up. The handbook itself
warns that it is not a rulebook and should be applied "with a grain of
salt"; treat these as defaults to test.

## The three metrics

- **CTR (click-through rate):** clicks divided by impressions. 10 million clicks on 100 million impressions is 10 percent. The thumbnail and title decide it, and it decides what videos get made.
- **AVD (average view duration):** how long viewers watch on average. The retention graph shows the exact second viewers leave.
- **AVP (average view percentage):** the share of the video watched.

The handbook compares two videos of almost the same length: the one
people watched 1 minute 38 seconds longer got about triple the views (120
million vs 45 million). A strong thumbnail only pays off if the video
holds the viewers it brings in.

## Picking an idea that can be packaged

- The same effort can produce very different CTR. "I Spent 50 Hours In My Front Yard" and "I Spent 50 Hours In Ketchup" cost about the same to make; the handbook calls the ketchup version "easily 100x more viral", because a person sitting in a bathtub of ketchup is far more interesting to look at than a front yard.
- Decide the thumbnail and title before you produce. If you cannot picture a thumbnail people would click, change the idea, not the thumbnail.
- Keep it simple. The audience is huge and worldwide, and an idea 50 million people understand at a glance has to be simple. The handbook's shorthand: "Me like simple."
- The core viewer, in Jimmy's words, is "a teenage memer that likes video games". Kids also watch with parents. Packaging should read for a young, global, phone-first audience.
- Original beats rehashed. Viewers say they want a format forever, then tire of it; channels that repeat formats for years die. Avoid packaging two videos from the same format back to back.

## Titles

- Pick the more extreme verb: "I Survived 50 Hours In Ketchup" adds intrigue over "I Spent". "Bananas Are The Worst Food On Earth" beats "I Don't Like Bananas".
- The title and thumbnail set the viewer's expectations, so they must match the video exactly. In the handbook's example, a "World's Largest Bouncy Castle" video whose thumbnail shows a giant yellow castle among tall buildings fails if the real castle is red, smaller, and in an empty field. Viewers feel lied to and leave.
- Never put the creator in a position where they have to lie. Nothing in the packaging should be fake.

## Formats that package well

- **Last to leave:** the winner and the prize are revealed only at the end, so the thumbnail shows the contest and the prize, never the winner.
- **Stair stepping:** the stakes rise through the video ($1, $10, $1,000, $100,000, then the world record). The thumbnail shows the top step or the contrast between the bottom and top steps.
- **Chase or escape:** will they get caught? The thumbnail shows the moment of pursuit.
- **Wow factor:** something no other creator can do (the handbook's example: lifting a house into the circle by crane 30 seconds into a 100-day challenge). It may not appear in the thumbnail, but it should appear early in the video.

## What the packaging promises the video must deliver

The thumbnail is a promise about the first minute. The handbook plans
retention by minute mark (its average video was about 13 minutes 37
seconds):

| Minute mark | Job |
| --- | --- |
| 0:00 to 1:00 | Most viewers leave here. Match the thumbnail and title immediately, light the opening well, script what is said, and front-load the best material. |
| 1:00 to 3:00 | Move from hype to showing. Use fast progression (cover several days, not day 1 in detail). |
| about 3:00 | A re-engagement: a spectacle that fits the story and impresses. |
| 3:00 to 6:00 | Simple, exciting content with quick scene changes so viewers fall for the story and the people. |
| about 6:00 | A second re-engagement that pushes the back half. |
| back half | Viewers are invested. Never signal the end except to build toward the payoff, then end abruptly. |

Use this table when filling in the "First 5 seconds" line of a variant,
and add a one-line retention plan when the user is planning a full video.

## Content rules that affect packaging

- You cannot fake intensity. A staged, forced face in the thumbnail that the video does not match reads as a lie.
- No dull moments. If the packaging promises one spectacle, the video needs more.
- Write backups. If an idea has a 50/50 chance of working, write another one; ideas are unlimited.
- Run packaging past as many people as you can before you lock it.
- Integrated sponsor segments keep retention; a scripted ad read creates a crater in the graph. This does not change the thumbnail, but it is part of keeping the promise.
