# Packaging rules

The full rulebook behind the skill. Grep for a heading instead of
reading the whole file.

## Why thumbnails matter

- The thumbnail is a billboard on the busiest highway there is. Unlike a billboard, you get click and retention numbers back within hours.
- More than 70 percent of YouTube views are on phones. A home-feed thumbnail is about 200x113 px. Design for a thumb, under a second of attention, and 30 other thumbnails on the same screen.
- A thumbnail has to look good and also get clicked. A "beautiful" thumbnail can lose to an "ugly" one that reads faster.
- MrBeast's production handbook names three numbers: CTR (clicks divided by impressions; 10 million clicks on 100 million impressions is 10 percent), AVD (average view duration), and AVP (average view percentage). CTR decides which videos get made. AVD and AVP decide how far YouTube pushes them: of two videos the same length, the one watched 1 minute 38 seconds longer got about triple the views (120 million vs 45 million).
- So optimize for clicks that turn into watch time. A high CTR with viewers leaving early still loses.

## Choosing an idea that packages well

- Effort is not the same as clickability. "I Spent 50 Hours In My Front Yard" and "I Spent 50 Hours In Ketchup" take about the same work; the handbook calls the ketchup one "easily 100x more viral".
- In general, the more extreme the better, as long as it is true.
- Keep it simple. For 50 million people to understand an idea at a glance, it has to be simple.
- Package for the real audience. MrBeast's is worldwide, includes kids watching with parents, and at its core is "a teenage memer that likes video games". Most YouTube and game audiences skew the same way.
- Viewers tire of formats they have seen too often, even ones they asked for. Rotate formats, and give a repeated format a visible new twist.
- The "wow factor" is something no one else could do. It often lives in the video (a house lowered in by crane 30 seconds in) instead of the thumbnail, but if the project has one, check whether it can be the thumbnail.

## The packaging philosophy

1. **Click first.** Plan the thumbnail and title before you make the video. They set the explicit promise.
2. **3-second rule.** On a small phone screen, a viewer must understand the premise and the tension in under 3 seconds.
3. **3-element limit.** No more than 3 visual elements (for example face, object, setting). More creates a visual traffic jam.
4. **Little or no text.** Cut it when the image already says it. If you use it, add context ("Day #2", "$10,000") rather than repeating what the image or title already says.

## The five patterns

1. **One focal subject.** One thing the eye lands on: a pile of money, a giant object, a face, a car on fire. Blur, darken, or crop everything else. The subject is the brightest, most saturated, most in-focus element.
2. **One exaggerated emotion.** Shock, triumph, disbelief, fear, joy. Dial it to 11. A slightly surprised face reads as nothing at feed size. The face should be at least 40 percent of the frame height. Pick one feeling: "losing my mind" reads at feed size, "surprised and confused" does not. A tight crop of any subject reacting (a pet, a character, a mascot) works the same way.
3. **A visible stake or number.** Money ($1,000,000), time (24 hours, 100 days), scale (world's largest), opposition (1 vs 100), or consequence (or I lose everything). Numbers should be at least 80 px tall on a 1280x720 export.
4. **Saturation and contrast for small screens.** Red, yellow, bright blue, deep black. Almost never muted tones. Use 1 or 2 hero colors on a complementary background.
5. **Minimal, specific text.** 2 to 4 words, concrete: $10,000, CURSED, WORLD'S FASTEST, 24 HOURS, DON'T BLINK. Never a sentence. Bold heavy sans-serif with a thick stroke or shadow. Write 5 to 10 options and pick the shortest that still carries the hook.

## The four archetypes

### Extreme scale or contrast

Compare opposite ends: a $1 item vs a $1,000,000 item, the smallest vs the
largest, day 1 vs day 100. Massively oversized or microscopically tiny
objects next to a person. Split-screen layouts work here, but keep each
half to one subject.

### Danger or tension

The viewer instantly wonders whether the subject is safe. A person
balanced over a drop, a timer at its last second, a monster right behind
the player. Show the moment before the outcome, never the outcome.

### Financial shock or extreme stakes

Physical cash, a giant check, a prize on a pedestal, a huge number. Make
the money tangible and enormous. Pair it with a face reacting to it.

### Bizarre action (the ketchup rule)

An unusual action is far more clickable than a normal one. "Sitting in a
bathtub of ketchup" beats "sitting in a front yard". Ask: what is the
strangest true thing in this video or project?

## Formats

The thumbnail asks the question the format answers at the end. Never
show the answer.

| Format | What happens | What the thumbnail shows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Last to leave | contestants endure until one wins a prize at the end | the contestants, the setting, and the prize |
| Stair stepping | the stakes rise step by step ($1, $10, $1,000, ... world record) | the top step, or the cheapest and most extreme side by side |
| Chase or escape | someone tries not to get caught | the pursuit at its closest moment |
| Versus | two sides compete | both sides, split frame, the prize between them |
| Survival over time | "100 days in..." | the subject in the hardest moment, with the day count |

## Faces

- Faces build trust and relationships over time, especially when the content keeps the thumbnail's promise.
- Shoot or generate several expression takes. You can tone one down later; you cannot rescue a subtle one.
- The feed is saturated with identical reaction faces. A distinct emotion (focused strain, smug confidence, quiet dread) can stand out more than another open mouth.

## Color guide

| Pairing | Feeling | Use for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Yellow on deep blue or purple | energy, value, fun | money, wins, challenges |
| Red on black | danger, urgency | survival, horror, deadlines |
| White on saturated red | alarm, breaking news | shock reveals |
| Cyan or teal vs orange | contrast, cinematic | two sides, versus, scale |
| Green on dark | money, success, nature | profit, growth, outdoors |

Lighting: strong rim light to separate the subject from the background,
bright key light on the face, darker or blurred background. Push
saturation and contrast past what feels tasteful on a monitor.

## Titles

- 50 characters or fewer. Title Case. Active voice. A clear question or a big hook.
- Pick the most extreme verb that is still true. "I Survived" adds intrigue over "I Spent". "Bananas Are The Worst Food On Earth" beats "I Don't Like Bananas".
- Title and thumbnail set the viewer's expectations, and the content has to meet them exactly. The handbook's example: a "World's Largest Bouncy Castle" thumbnail shows a giant yellow castle next to tall buildings. If the video opens on a smaller red castle in an empty field, viewers feel lied to and leave. Nothing in the packaging should be fake.
- Title and thumbnail work as a pair: the title adds what the image cannot show; the image shows what the title cannot say. Never duplicate them.
- Patterns that work: "I Survived 100 Days In...", "$1 vs $1,000,000 ...", "Last To Leave ... Wins ...", "I Built ... In 24 Hours", "World's Largest ...".

## Testing

- Change one variable at a time (face, stake, color, text) so you know what moved the number.
- YouTube Studio's "Test and compare" runs up to 3 thumbnails against each other and picks by watch time, which is the right metric.
- Treat every rule here as a default to test. MrBeast's team switched many thumbnails from open-mouth to closed-mouth faces when the data said so, and his handbook asks readers to apply it "with a grain of salt".
- Run drafts past as many people as you can before locking them, and make backups: if an idea has a 50/50 chance of working, write another.
- The `thumbnail.py --preview` image is a quick check on a monitor. Before publishing, also look at the final thumbnail on a real phone, dimmed, at arm's length. If you cannot tell what is happening, neither can the viewer.

## Keeping the promise

Most viewers who leave do it in the first minute, so the first minute is
where the packaging is proven or broken. Show the thumbnail's object,
color, and setting right away, light the opening well, and do not spend
the first minute explaining what is coming. Then keep the stakes rising
and save the final answer (the winner, the top step, the result) for the
end. Anyone making the content needs to know the thumbnail and title
before they start, so the opening matches them.

## Make it your own

MrBeast's thumbnail lead Chucky Appleby put it this way: "The person who
ends up being the quote, unquote 'next MrBeast' is going to be doing
something totally different." Use these rules, then give the thumbnail
its own identity: a signature color, a recurring character, a layout
viewers learn to recognize.
